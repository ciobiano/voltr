Architects & Spatial Principles

Tadao Ando — Silence Through Concrete

Japan — Church of the Light, Naoshima Museum, Water Temple

Spatial concept

Ando's concrete is not cold — it's silent. The material recedes so light and space can speak. His architecture is defined by what it removes. Walls are thick, openings are few and precise. Light enters only through deliberate cuts.

Pattern to extract

  • Material recedes, light speaks — the background should be a quiet container, not a statement
  • Light through absence — bright moments in UI are "cuts" through a dark surface
  • Thick boundaries — generous padding and margins create the feeling of "thick walls" between sections
  • Slow light transitions — Ando's light changes over hours. UI light should shift slowly (400-800ms ease-in-out)

VOLTR application

Dark backgrounds with precise, deliberate "cuts" of bright content. Wide margins between sections feel like walking through thick walls. Color transitions are slow and atmospheric.

Peter Zumthor — Atmosphere & Material Presence

Switzerland — Therme Vals, Bruder Klaus Field Chapel, Kolumba Museum

Spatial concept

Zumthor designs for the senses. Materials are chosen for how they feel, smell, and age — not just how they look. Therme Vals uses local quartzite laid in horizontal bands that reference geological strata. Light is never direct; it's always filtered, bounced, or diffused.

Pattern to extract

  • Material-first design — choose the "material" (visual texture, color, surface quality) before the form
  • Atmosphere over information — the feeling of a space matters more than the objects in it
  • Reflected / filtered light — no direct hard light in UI. Everything should bounce or diffuse
  • Aging and depth — materials have history. UI elements can have subtle depth that suggests permanence

VOLTR application

UI "materials" chosen before layout. Atmosphere as a design input — the page mood is set before content is placed. All shadows are diffused and soft.

David Chipperfield — Precision & Restraint

UK/Germany — Neues Museum reconstruction, Modern Museum of Art Stockholm

Spatial concept

Chipperfield's work is about reduction to essence. The Neues Museum restoration leaves bomb damage visible — history is part of the material. His spaces are restrained but never cold. Proportion is everything.

Pattern to extract

  • Remove until it breaks — then add back one thing
  • History as material — the context of a section (how user arrived) should be visible in the transition
  • Proportion system — every dimension relates to every other (modular scale)

VOLTR application

Strict modular scale for all dimensions. Section transitions acknowledge where the user came from.

Kengo Kuma — Material Lightness

Japan — V&A Dundee, GC Prostho Museum, Japan National Stadium

Spatial concept

Kuma uses materials in unexpected ways — stone as curtain, wood as lattice, light as fabric. His buildings feel porous, not solid. There's always a threshold where inside and outside blur.

Pattern to extract

  • Porous boundaries — section dividers don't have to be solid lines. They can be gradients, fog, or light
  • Thresholds as spaces — transitions between sections are themselves designed moments, not afterthoughts
  • Material reimagined — use UI elements in unexpected ways (a border that becomes a shadow, a shadow that becomes content)

VOLTR application

No hard section dividers (lines, borders). Transitions are fog, light, or gradient changes. The space between sections is designed as carefully as the sections themselves.

Foster + Partners — High-Tech Precision

UK — Gherkin, Willis Building, Hearst Tower, Apple Park influence

Spatial concept

Foster's work is engineering as aesthetic. Structure is expressed, not hidden. Every joint, every panel, every seam is intentionally aligned. Technology is celebrated, not concealed.

Pattern to extract

  • Engineering as aesthetic — expose the system state, don't hide it behind decoration
  • Grid precision — every element is on the grid. Nothing is approximate.
  • Structure expressed — navigation, loading states, transitions can be visible as "structure"

VOLTR application

Loading states and transitions are honest — the user sees the system working. Strict grid alignment everywhere. No "organic" or approximate positioning.

SANAA — Weightless Minimalism

Japan — New Museum (NYC), Rolex Learning Center, Kanagawa Institute of Technology

Spatial concept

SANAA designs buildings that barely seem to exist. White, thin, translucent. Boundaries dissolve. The Rolex Learning Center is one continuous space with no doors — you flow through it.

Pattern to extract

  • Weightless UI — elements that feel like they could float away
  • Continuous flow — no hard section breaks. Content flows seamlessly.
  • Translucency — see-through elements that suggest what's next

VOLTR application

Use sparingly — for transition moments and "previews" of next section. Translucent overlays that hint at content beneath.

John Pawson — Absolute Reduction

UK — Nový Dvůr Monastery, minimalism and plainness as philosophy

Spatial concept

Pawson takes minimalism to its extreme. A room is a floor, four walls, and light. Nothing else. The absence of objects becomes the experience.

Pattern to extract

  • Plainness as luxury — not having something is itself a feature
  • Material purity — one material per space. No mixing.
  • Light as content — when there's nothing else, light becomes the subject

VOLTR application

Some sections can be almost empty — just a headline, generous space, and atmospheric light. Not every section needs data or interaction.

Álvaro Siza — Poetic Modernism

Portugal — Serralves Museum, Expo '98 Portuguese Pavilion

Spatial concept

Siza's buildings feel inevitable — they belong exactly where they are. White forms that catch Mediterranean light. Simple geometries that feel deeply considered.

Pattern to extract

  • Inevitable placement — every element should feel like it couldn't be anywhere else
  • Light geometry — simple forms (rectangle, circle, line) used with precision
  • Site-specific — the design responds to its context

VOLTR application

Every UI element has a reason for its position. Simple geometries used consistently. The page responds to its "context" — scroll position, time, user behavior.
